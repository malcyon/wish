# records

Censuses and cross-checks over character records that span titles and ports, such as class combinations, fields and encumbrance.

| file | purpose |
|---|---|
| `boundarychars.py` | Deliberately extreme *Pool of Radiance* characters for `tests/test_boundary.py`. Every case is a `NeutralCharacter` read as though off a C64 disk, which is what a real conversion into DOS sees. A real party rarely sits near a limit, so the records that exist can say whether a conversion is faithful and cannot say whether it is safe. |
| `carryceiling.py` | Counts how close any real character gets to the two limits a conversion might have to tell a player about -- the sixteen item slots in a C64 record and the ten trait slots at `0x0AD`, shared between racial effects and item grants. `.claude/rules/conversions.md` says a chooser is not built until a measurement says it is needed, and this is that measurement: it sweeps every C64 disk, DOS archive, played save directory, specimen and Amiga disk image on the machine, all three ports and all titles, and reports the widest inventory and the fullest trait list per port, per title and per provenance grade. 950 characters on 2026-09-07: exactly one carries more than sixteen items, and it is the specimen this project built to make that happen; nothing anywhere wants more than five of the ten trait slots. `--json` writes one row per character, `--grants` the effect-granting item templates, `--items` the DOS `item_count` histogram (`#399 (A conversion that runs out of item or trait slots tells the player nothing, because the pane never shows a warning)`). |
| `classcodecensus.py` | Counts the records whose class **code** disagrees with the classes they hold levels in, per port and per title, which is how `#310 (A trained C64 Curse character arrives in DOS with the wrong class on his sheet)` was measured. The bits-to-code table is the game's own, read out of Curse's C64 `GEN` at `$1951`; the mask is derived from the level array rather than from `class_bits`, because a dual-classed character's mask names two classes while the code names the one he is, and DOS's stored mask is folded through `goldbox.dos_codec.neutral_class_bits` first, since DOS numbers the paladin's and the ranger's bits differently from the C64. 8 of 30 C64 Curse records disagree and every one of the eight is a character Curse's own engine trained or dual-classed; 0 of 162 DOS Curse and Silver Blades records do, and the four Pool of Radiance disagreements in the archives are all SILAS, whose level array carries a thief 1 his mask and his code have never heard of. Reads the specimen tree and, with `--archives`, the player's archives; writes nothing. |
| `classcombocensus.py` | Counts, per port and title, which class combinations the character corpus holds and how many records store a dual-class old level, over the specimen tree, the Pool disks and the shipped archives plus any `--c64-root` and `--dos-root` given, for `#345 (Draw a letter in each combat-map square saying what is standing there, instead of the index the backend counts with)`. Counts what exists and is not evidence about the game. Read only. |
| `classcombocheck.py` | Asks, of every record it is pointed at, whether the editor's Class combo would draw the class a conversion writes -- `#393 (A dual-classed Curse character may show one class in the editor and convert as another, and no specimen exists to tell)`. It calls the shipped code on both sides, `editor.window._char_class_shown` and `goldbox.c64_codec.read`, so a change to either moves what it prints, and it shows the stored `class_bits` beside the bitmask the level array implies, those two being equal on the C64 is why the two answers agree. A DOS record is converted through `goldbox.dos_codec.to_neutral` and `goldbox.c64_codec.write` first, because `editor/roster.py` opens a `.d64` and nothing else, so the combo rule never sees a raw DOS record. 0 disagreements in 132 C64 records and 228 converted DOS ones in the specimen tree, 12 of them dual-classed. Exits 1 on a disagreement; writes nothing. |
| `classlegality.py` | Which classes each race may take **at character creation**, read off both ports of Pool of Radiance -- the table `goldbox/levels.py` does not carry, and not the same one as the racial *level* limit: the racial row gives a dwarf a cleric limit of 8, and no dwarf is ever offered CLERIC. The C64's `GEN` menu builder is `LDA <race bits>,Y` then `AND <legality>,X`, so both the race-bit table's address and the one-byte-per-class-code legality mask's come out of its own operands rather than being fitted by hand; DOS keeps the same matrix as seven counted, strictly increasing class-code lists found by content. On this machine's disks the two agree on all 37 entries over the 7 races -- legality at C64 `$0E64`, the DOS lists at `START.EXE 0x00f44f` -- which is what answered `#510 (Can a Pool of Radiance character memorise more than the 21 spells its DOS record allots?)`: **only a half-elf may be a cleric/magic-user**, so only a half-elf can hold both spell lists at once. Reads the player's own disks and archives; with neither port present it prints nothing and exits 0. |
| `compare.py` | Loads every character specimen we have and reports, per record offset, how much the value varies — offsets that vary are candidate fields, and correlating that variation with attributes we already know is how the rest get identified. No emulator needed. |
| `controlbyte.py` | The Gold Box **control byte** on both ports at once: C64 `0x0B8` and DOS `0x084` (`0x0F7`, `0x0FF`, `0x147` in the later titles), the byte whose bit 7 says the engine drives this character rather than the player. `tools/dos/dosbyteimm.py` reads what an engine *stores* there; this reports what the records on this machine *hold*, partitioned by value with the reading beside each -- the low seven bits are a morale percentage stored halved, so `$B2` prints as morale 100. It is what settled the last UNKNOWN of `#303 (The DOS record may hold the NPC flag that the conversion reports as having nowhere to go)`: 5 engine-driven C64 records against 1 DOS one (OUGO, `$B2`), and 291 of 297 C64 and 457 of 458 DOS records at `$00` or `$01`. The DOS offset comes out of each title's own shape -- fourth byte from the end of `field_83_87` -- rather than a per-title table, so it reproduces the four offsets `tools/dos/dosbyteimm.py` finds the compares at. The DOS half reuses `tools/dos/dostailcensus.py`'s finder and its exclusions; `--disk` adds a C64 image outside the registry, which is how a save with companions in it gets counted. Reads only. |
| `enccensus.py` | The `money + Σ(weight × quantity)` encumbrance identity swept over every DOS and Amiga record on the machine, each graded by where it was found and by whether its item file is even present; `--stacks` finds the item stacks whose cached display line disagrees with the quantity byte, and `--pair` diffs two save directories one game action apart. Written for `#323 (The encumbrance identity does not survive the training fee, so failing it is not evidence of an edited record)`. |
| `fieldcensus.py` | One named record field, over every character record on this machine -- what `thac0census.py` does for `thac0_base`, asked of any field by name and knowing nothing about what the answer should be. Three corpora, each named in the header line because mixing them is how a census lies: `c64` (the player's save disks plus the title's own specimen disks), `dos` (the specimen tree, the archives and the player's folder, with `--all-titles`), and `monsters`, which nothing else reads -- Pool of Radiance's 116 `MON<hex>` files are PRGs whose body is a character record at offset 0, so a monster's `level` and `attack_level` come out through the ordinary layout. It settled `#527 (A DOS import combines saving throws from classes the character does not have)`'s last open byte: `attack_level` at `0x098` is the fighter's level in 26 of 26 C64 records that have one and in 22 of the 23 `MON*` templates that carry one -- `8TH LVL FIGHTER` holds 8 -- while 220 of 238 DOS Pool of Radiance records hold the **constant 1**, a party taken to fighter 8 through the game's own schools included. Curse and Silver Blades do maintain the byte, which is why the answer could not be read off one corpus. The C64 sweep is by filename prefix rather than by directory, because `por-c64/` in the specimen tree holds six Curse and six Silver Blades disks and globbing it whole adds six records of another game's layout. Nothing is written; the disks are opened read-only. |
| `geoplausible.py` | Re-takes the measurement behind `automap.area.looks_like_a_map` (`#436 (The map plausibility check throws out five of Pool of Radiance's own maps)`). `maps` scores every `GEO` file and `GEO.GLB` block on the disks this machine has; `sweep` scores every 1024-byte window of every other file on them, which is what the thresholds are calibrated to reject; `thresholds` prints, for each constant, the worst real map beside the best non-map that clears the *other* three clauses, which is the only honest way to state a margin. It imports `map_evidence` rather than recomputing anything, so the tool cannot disagree with the code it is measuring. Two traps it remembers for you: excluding maps by filename misses the Amiga's `/SAVE/spindisk`, which holds all sixteen Curse maps verbatim, and sweeping a raw disk image catches sector-shifted fragments that cannot be excluded at all. |
| `geoports.py` | Diffs the same Gold Box map between the ports that shipped it (`#443 (Three of Curse's sixteen maps differ between the C64 and the Amiga, and nobody has looked at how)`). `diff` pairs the C64 `GEO<id>` files, the Amiga `GEO.GLB` library and the DOS `GEO<n>.DAX` blocks by area id and decodes every differing byte -- which plane, which square, and whether it is wall art, a barrier, a roofed bit or a script id. `closest` measures both sides of `automap.area.NEAR_ENOUGH`: the widest gap between two ports' copies of one area, which the tolerance must reach, and the closest two *different* places, which it must stay under -- and exits non-zero when it does not. `blocks` is the census of every DOS `GEO<n>.DAX` block in the archives, one row each: the two bytes in front, and the four quantities `looks_like_a_map` measures, printed rather than filtered on. A DOS block is 1026 bytes and the engine's `Load3DMap` copies four planes out of it from offset 2, so the reader takes any block of that size and trims two -- `00 04` is there in four of the six titles because their maps came from the same source as a C64 release, and Treasures of the Savage Frontier and Pools of Darkness carry `00 00` and `cc dd` instead (`#466 (The DOS map reader keeps only blocks with a C64 load address, so two titles' maps are invisible)`). |
| `infravision.py` | Where a Gold Box character's infravision comes from, and what a conversion does with it -- the tool behind the measurement that settled the last line on `#52 (File ▸ Import and File ▸ Export for every direction the library supports)`'s C64 → DOS rows. `table` reads the race-indexed table the C64's own character generator writes record `0x0D5` from, off the player's `GEN` overlay, and prints the four instructions that use it: Pool of Radiance's `$0E5C` and Curse of the Azure Bonds' `$0C4B` both hold 6, 6, 6, 6, **3**, 6, 0 for races 1-7, so the halfling's thirty feet is the game's own number and not the sixty AD&D would predict (`#392 (A converted halfling gets sixty feet of infravision, where the C64's own generator gives him thirty)`). `read` prints every occupied slot of a save with its race, its stored byte and what the generator would have written. `stage` copies a save disk and sets the byte to a value each character's race does *not* imply -- a dwarf to 0, a human to 6 -- which is what makes a load-and-resave in the running game able to say whether the engine recomputes it; staging a value the race would have produced anyway proves nothing. Nothing here writes to the player's disks. |
| `spellbookcensus.py` | Every character record on this machine, on all three ports, partitioned by what its **book of known spells** holds -- and in particular whether anything sets Pool of Radiance's spell id 56, `RESTORATION`, which the DOS record has a byte for at `0x06A` and the C64's seven-byte mask at `0x078` has no bit for. It is what answered `#411 (Nobody knows whether a converted cleric loses Restoration, because the spellbook field is one bit short of the game's own spell list)`: **0 of 567** Pool of Radiance records set it, in the book or in the memorised list, and every one of their 141 clerics carries a book, so the field is not the magic-user's alone. `--control` re-asks the same question of every DOS record file on the machine raw -- no deduplication, no exclusions -- which is what makes "nothing anywhere has it" safe: 2,891 files, 1,808 of them Pool of Radiance, and all 160 hits Pools of Darkness, whose id space is a different game's. Reads the C64 mask *past* the width the title uses, so a bit set where the game does not look is visible rather than invisible; reads the memorised list at each title's own width from `tools/c64/memorisedwidth.py`'s measurement rather than the shared 69-byte field; and picks an Amiga saved game's container with `goldbox.amiga_savegame.detect` rather than trying each in turn, because the record signature is identical in both later titles and the wrong container yields six plausible characters rather than an error. Takes the **parent** of each registered disk directory too, so the three C64 titles with no `gamedisks.yaml` entry are read rather than silently missed, and flags a title `goldbox/spells.py` has no table for instead of measuring its book against Pool of Radiance's ceiling. Every row is graded by where it was found -- `built`, `spec`, `edited`, `found` -- over **all** the paths a deduplicated record turned up at, since the specimen tree is the one with provenance behind it. Reads only. |
| `thac0census.py` | Every stored `thac0_base` on this machine against the port's own THAC0 table, which is what showed that the two ports disagree about a low-level magic-user or thief because they **ship different tables**, not because either clamps or caches. `tables` prints both side by side, `c64` and `dos` sweep every record. Neither table is hardcoded: the C64's comes off whichever `POOL*.D64` carries `GEN`, and the DOS one out of the EXEPACK-expanded `START.EXE`, located by the **class-bit run** that sits immediately after it -- eight bytes, `02 20 08 40 80 01 04 10`, occurring exactly once -- so the read cannot agree with `goldbox/levels.py` by construction. `code` prints every `mov dx,<stride> / mul dx / ... / mov al,[di+off]` site in `GAME.OVR`, which is where the row width and the table's DS offset come from rather than a guess; Pool of Radiance's four all read `mul 11` at `DS:0x3C7C`. 190 of 190 DOS records and 148 of 158 C64 records reproduce, and all ten C64 misses are on the five disks Wish itself converted from DOS -- `#318 (DOS gives a low-level magic-user or thief THAC0 20 where the C64 gives 21, and our table holds only the C64's)` and `#366 (A converted magic-user or thief arrives with the other port's THAC0, because the two ports ship different tables and the conversion copies the byte)`. Reads only. |
| `thiefskillcensus.py` | Every stored thief skill on this machine against the port's own tables, which is what showed that the C64 and DOS builds of Pool of Radiance **ship different racial adjustment rows** -- the C64's is the DOS one a byte short from the gnome's hear-noise column on, so its gnome, half-elf, halfling and half-orc thieves each read a row displaced one column. `tables` prints all three blocks per port, `rows` puts the racial rows side by side and measures the byte-stream shift between them, `c64` and `dos` sweep every record. Neither side is hardcoded: the C64's come off whichever disk carries `GEN`, and the DOS ones out of the EXEPACK-expanded `START.EXE`, located by **the C64's own 72 bytes of level table**, which occurs exactly once in each image -- so the read cannot agree with `goldbox/levels.py` by construction. Two structural checks say the geometry is right: the human racial row must be zeros and the dexterity rows for 13, 14 and 15 must adjust nothing. It also measures the rule each port runs: 59 of 63 DOS records reproduce as level + race + **dexterity**, clamped at zero, and 27 of 27 engine-written C64 records as level + race with no dexterity at all, matching `GEN $1FEC`. `--rule` says which rule each record fits. Reads only. |
| `traitnames.py` | What a title's own shipped data says an effect code means, beside the name `goldbox/traits.py` gives it. Two of the routes `docs/171-c64-trait-slots.md` grades are censuses of shipped data rather than reads of code, and this puts each one against the table. **The spell audit** (the default) reads the per-spell record through `tools.c64.traitquery.spell_effects` -- `ECL65 +0` at seven bytes a record in Pool of Radiance, `COMBAT2 +2732` and `+2937` at nine in the later two -- and prints every code one of the title's own spells writes, the spells that write it, the combat message and the spell group beside it, and what our table calls that code today. The group column is what makes a row readable: `COMBAT2` gives one string to a spell granted at two levels, so a shared name is ordinary, but a row whose name pointer was never set reads as the table's first string and writes an id that has nothing to do with it -- Curse has fourteen such rows and Silver Blades twenty, and `docs/171-c64-trait-slots.md`'s "A spell row is not evidence until its name is its own" is the rule. **`--monsters`** reads the ten bytes at record `0x0AD` out of every `MON<hex>` template on the title's sides and reports which codes are carried and by what, with `--against TITLE` putting another title's carriers for the same code beside them and `--records` printing one row per template instead. That is the route most of `NAMES` was built on, and run against a second title it refuses as well as confirms. It settled both pre-steps on `#561 (A Curse of the Azure Bonds character's traits are named from Pool of Radiance's table, which disagrees with Curse's own data about eight codes)`: 47 codes are written by a Curse spell, 43 of them by a row in a spell group, of which 32 agree with `NAMES`, 8 contradict it and 3 (136 Entangle, 142 Fear, 143 Fire Shield) have no name in it at all; and Curse's 70 `MON*` templates carry 52 distinct codes, **none of them 68 or 69**, with thirteen codes landing on the same creature in both titles, twelve landing on a creature the Pool of Radiance name cannot describe, and eight that `NAMES` does not name. The reader checks itself against the published Pool of Radiance census -- 116 templates, the `$FF` fill in 38 of them, 121 on AHNKHEG, 127 on BASILISK and MEDUSA -- and gives Silver Blades the 71 templates `docs/171-c64-trait-slots.md` already carries. Neither route names a code that no spell writes and no creature carries; `tools/c64/traitquery.py --handlers` is the one that does, and is the tie-breaker when these two disagree. Needs no emulator and no save; the disks are opened read-only. |
| `turncensus.py` | Every C64 character record on the machine with the turning byte at `0x0A4` beside the one the title's own `GEN` would compute from its cleric and paladin levels, which is what says a converter may compute the byte rather than copy it. 210 records, 187 agreeing, and **all 23 disagreements a converted party this project wrote** -- the clearest being `WISH-SPEC-curse-trained-party`, where the two characters Curse's own hall trained carry the trainer's 7 and 5 while the untrained paladin still holds the conversion's zero. Exits non-zero when any record disagrees, so it is a check as much as a listing. `--dos` asks the other half: which DOS records carry anything at `0x076`, which is the **undead's** row rather than the caster's -- eleven monster records in Pool of Radiance, every one undead, and no player character anywhere. `docs/178-turning-undead.md`, `#288 (A converted cleric or paladin arrives on the C64 unable to turn undead, because DOS keeps no turning byte and nothing computes one)`. |
